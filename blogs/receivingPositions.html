Receiving Usage and Efficiency by Position

September 2024

Wide receivers, tight ends, and running backs split the targets in every NFL offense, but they do completely different jobs. A position-by-position look at 17,540 targets from 2023.

Splitting the pie

Every 2023 pass attempt with a listed receiver is tagged with that player's position (fullbacks counted as running backs). Wide receivers dominate the volume, but the more interesting result is how each position's share changes from targets to yards to touchdowns.

Running backs have the highest catch rate in football at 77%, so their share of receptions (21.5%) is bigger than their share of targets (18.7%). Those catches are short, though, and RBs end up with just 14.1% of receiving yards and 12.5% of receiving touchdowns. Wide receivers go the other way: 59.7% of targets becomes 64.6% of the yards.

These shares are very stable. From 2020 through 2023, the WR share of targets never moved outside 59.5–59.7%, TEs stayed at 21–22%, and RBs at about 19%. League-wide, offenses split the ball almost identically every season, even as individual teams vary a lot.

Where each position is targeted

The real difference between positions is depth. Figure 2 shows where along the field each position's targets are thrown, measured as air yards beyond the line of scrimmage at the catch point.

  • 58% of RB targets are at or behind the line of scrimmage, vs. 17% for TEs and 13% for WRs.
  • 17% of WR targets travel 20+ air yards. Only 6% of TE targets and 1% of RB targets do.
  • Average depth of target (aDOT): WR 10.8, TE 6.4, RB −0.1 yards.

How each position gains its yards

That depth difference determines how each position produces yards. On an average catch, a wide receiver's yards come mostly from the throw itself. A running back's reception starts behind the line, so every yard comes after the catch.

Running backs lead every position with 7.8 yards after catch per reception, nearly twice a wide receiver's 4.4. It isn't enough, though: an RB reception gains 7.1 yards in total, vs. 10.2 for tight ends and 12.6 for wide receivers.

The players

Figure 4 places every player with 50+ targets on the same two axes, depth of target and YAC. The positions separate into three clear groups, with a few hybrids in between: Deebo Samuel and Rashee Rice are WRs used like running backs, George Kittle is a TE who does both, and Christian McCaffrey's 1.8-yard aDOT is among the deepest of any running back. 

targets = pbp[(pbp.season_type == "REG") & (pbp.pass_attempt == 1)
              & (pbp.sack == 0) & pbp.receiver_player_id.notna()]

# One position per player-season, from the weekly player stats
pos = (player_stats.groupby(["season", "player_id"]).position
       .agg(lambda s: s.mode().iat[0]).replace({"FB": "RB"}).rename("pos"))
targets = targets.join(pos, on=["season", "receiver_player_id"])

by_pos = targets.groupby("pos").agg(
    targets=("epa", "size"),
    catch_rate=("complete_pass", "mean"),
    adot=("air_yards", "mean"),
    epa_per_target=("epa", "mean"),
)

Are running-back targets bad?

Per target, the passing game strongly prefers wide receivers (+0.21 EPA) and tight ends (+0.19) over running backs (−0.05). That's the analytics case against "feeding the back in the passing game": a typical RB target loses value, and a typical WR target adds it.

But comparing positions overall mixes together who is targeted and where. Figure 5 holds depth constant.

Throws behind the line of scrimmage lose value for everyone (−0.14 to −0.24 EPA), and RBs are actually the best of the three there. Screens to receivers and tight ends fare worse. Running backs do lag at 0–9 yards (+0.00 vs. +0.12 for WRs), partly because many of those are check-downs on plays where nothing else was open.

The bigger effect is the mix. If RB targets were spread across depths the way WR targets are, using the RB EPA at each depth, they'd be worth about +0.19 EPA per target, close to the WR average. Most of the efficiency gap comes from where backs are targeted, not from backs being worse receivers. Running backs are a good outlet when the play breaks down, but an offense built on RB targets is an offense built on low-value throws.

Is yards after catch a skill?

Running backs lead the league in YAC, but that's mostly about where they catch the ball: a screen behind the line comes with blockers and open field. To separate the player from the situation, nflfastR includes an expected YAC model (xyac_mean_yardage). It estimates how many yards after the catch an average receiver would gain on each completion, given the catch depth, field position, down, and distance. YAC over expected (YACOE) is the gap between the two. 

comp = targets[targets.complete_pass == 1].dropna(subset=["yards_after_catch", "xyac_mean_yardage"])
comp["yacoe"] = comp.yards_after_catch - comp.xyac_mean_yardage

players = (comp.groupby(["receiver_player_name", "pos", "posteam"])
               .agg(rec=("yacoe", "size"),
                    yac=("yards_after_catch", "mean"),
                    xyac=("xyac_mean_yardage", "mean"),
                    yacoe=("yacoe", "mean"))
               .query("rec >= 50")
               .sort_values("yacoe", ascending=False))

Adjusting for situation flips the position story. Running backs averaged 7.9 YAC per catch against an expected 7.7, so only +0.2 over expected. Tight ends were at −0.1 and wide receivers at −0.5. The xyac model runs slightly high for 2023 overall, so these are best read relative to each other: RBs' big YAC numbers are almost entirely the situations they're thrown into, not extra elusiveness.

The top of the list is a who's who of after-the-catch players: Deebo Samuel (+3.2 yards per catch), Rashee Rice (+3.0), and George Kittle (+2.9), the same hybrids that stood out in Figure 4. It also shows how scheme and skill mix: two of the top three play for Kyle Shanahan, whose offense is built to create space after the catch. The bottom five are all wide receivers, each losing about two yards per catch against what the situation offered.

Does it repeat from year to year?

A skill should persist. Pairing every receiver with 40+ catches in one season with his next season (2020→21 through 2024→25, 321 player pairs) shows how stable each number is:

The most stable numbers describe a player's role: depth of target (r = 0.90) and expected YAC (0.86) barely change, because they're set by how the offense uses him. Raw YAC per catch looks fairly stable (0.66), but most of that is the role carrying over. YAC over expected, the part that's actually the player, repeats at only r = 0.36. There's a real skill in there, but one season of it is mostly noise, so a big YACOE season should be expected to come back toward average.

Caveats

  • Position labels are roster positions. A "WR" lined up in the backfield (Deebo Samuel) is still counted as a WR.
  • EPA per target credits the receiver for the whole play, including the quarterback's decision and the defense's coverage.
  • The depth-mix calculation uses tiny RB samples at 10+ yards, so treat the +0.19 as "about the same as a WR," not as a precise figure.

Data and code

Play-by-play and weekly player stats from nflverse. A target is any non-sack pass attempt with a listed receiver. The year-to-year comparison uses 2020–2025. Every number and chart comes from analysis/receiving_positions.py.
